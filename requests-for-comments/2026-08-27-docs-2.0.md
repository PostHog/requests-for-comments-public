# Request for comments: Docs 2 Electric Boogaloo – Sarah Sanders

## Problem statement

It's no secret that docs aren't the same anymore. Humans are reading less, and using agents more. This doesn't mean docs are _dead_, we still very much need them as a living product reference. It means: _the shape and purpose of "docs as we know them" has changed._

When I pulled the numbers for this RFC in August, visits to `posthog.com/docs` averaged **2.12 pages**, down from 2.86 in Q3 2025 and 3.02 in Q3 2024. That's down 26% year over year.

At the same time, way more people are visiting:

- 87.6K unique visitors to `/docs` in July 2025
- 151.5K in July 2026

That's +73% visitors for +19% pageviews. More people are arriving to docs, but each one is reading less.

And tutorials are worse: 46K pageviews in March 2025, 23K in July 2026.

This doesn't tell us *why* every person reads less. It does give us a reason to rethink what we're asking them to read.

### So what do we do?

We're due for a new era of docs. I'm calling this new shape "docs for humans". In my mind, there's:

- **Docs for robots:** Simple, crawlable references and downloadable skills. These are where product knowledge lives, and the purpose is for agents to use it as context. Humans can still look things up here too.
- **Docs for humans:** Docs written for humans! Use case guides, interactive components, example apps, hands-on labs. These are designed entirely with humans in mind, because humans still _want_ to read and learn, they just don't want to read an info dump of reference material.

These belong together. You learn the concept, try it, then hand your agent the skills to apply it to your own project. And, crucially, you know enough to tell whether it did a good job.

So we're building Docs 2.0 for _humans and agents_. But what do humans actually need?

It's not a list of formats, it's a list of _jobs_. Here's a model I am proposing. It's inspired by a bunch of different models out there, but tailored for why I think PostHog users visit docs:

- **1 – Look it up:** _What's that thing called and how do I install something?_. Our reference docs handle this, and agents increasingly do the reading. We need to make those references easier to use and maintain as skills.
- **2 – Size it up:** _Is this the right tool for me?_ Someone is evaluating PostHog or coming from another tool. They need to understand how it works, what decisions they'll have to make, and what it does on real data. This overlaps substantially with marketing-owned evaluation and comparison content, so Docs should focus on the deeper product education: explaining how PostHog works, what technical decisions are involved, and how it behaves on real data. Marketing owns positioning, messaging, and broad comparison content; Docs should support that work rather than duplicate it.
- **3 – Get it:** _I don't understand how this thing works._ This is the big one for us and it's getting bigger. Self-driving, context warehouse, signals: these are _new mental models_. Our comprehension is being outpaced by how fast we ship.
- **4 – Mess around:** _Let me poke at it before I commit._ Low barrier to entry, click things, see what happens. Our current version of this is sign up with the wizard and instrument your app, which is still a huge ask for someone who is still deciding. And while it gives people something to click around in, it still doesn't teach them much.
- **5 – Get good:** _I want to be great at this, not just get it working._ The gap between someone who has PostHog installed and someone who is a power user is enormous. This includes knowing what to ask your agent, and how to spot when it's wrong.
- **6 – Unbreak it:** _Something is broken and I'm on fire._ No patience, wants the answer, right now. Agents are decent at this, but only if the docs contain the actual failure modes, and ours don't have good coverage here.

We can do the best marketing in the world, but if we don't focus on _teaching_ people – how to use PostHog, why it's important, how to get the most out of the platform – we become a company that innovates and lets our users try to keep up. Even the frontier labs have been [hiring](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/anthropic/jobs/5288959008) people to come in and lead user education.

Education is the important word there. **Docs 2.0 is education.** It isn't docs with nicer components and it isn't a content refresh. We build the thing that _actually_ teaches people PostHog in the concrete. Every proposal in this RFC flows from this premise.

Anthropic's Education job posting put it better than I can:

> "The gap between what Claude can do and what developers know how to build with it is widening."

Swap Claude for PostHog and that's our problem, word for word. We ship faster than people can learn us, self-driving gives them even more to understand, and you cannot reference-doc your way out of a comprehension problem.

Also, we live in the era of _anything_, yes ANYTHING, is possible. Let's get weird with it and push it as far as we can go.

## What I'm proposing

**Build an educational platform in Q4.** The self-driving pocket guide was the beginning. We have more pocket guides now, and Learn is already integrated into the [AIO docs](https://posthog.com/docs/ai-observability). That gave us the foundation. Now: how do we take it to the next level?

Learn becomes the place you choose how to learn PostHog:
- Read a use case.
- Explore an app.
- Build something in a lab.
- See how it applies to your project.
- Take a bundle of skills back to your agent.

The pieces should fit together, and you should be able to move between them.

I'm also folding [the Twig proposal](https://github.com/PostHog/requests-for-comments-public/pull/593) into this. Twig becomes our deployed, self-driving example app, built from Unter. It gives the educational content something real to teach from, and the labs something familiar to practice on.

This is my main project for Q4: the platform, Twig, connected learning content, the skills library, personalization, and the knowledge agent that helps us turn Sales' questions into better content. Self-driving comes first, followed by the tools that give it useful signals.

## Success criteria

The question is still: _did people get better at PostHog?_ That applies to our users, their agents, and our own teams.

We should measure it. Charles is right that this needs numbers, and Edwin is right that people sharing something, praising it, or coming back with suggestions matters too. I don't want to invent a 40% completion target before we know what a useful learning experience looks like.

### Did they learn something?

- **Can they do the thing?** In a lab, can someone fix a targeting rule or explain why a self-driving PR does or doesn't solve the problem? We track attempts, successful checks, resets, and where they get stuck. Clicking “next” isn't the same as getting it.
- **Do they keep going?** Track active reading, use of the interactive examples, movement between learning paths, and return visits. Someone spending longer on a page might be engaged, or they might be confused. We need their feedback too.
- **Ask them.** Embed questions like “did this teach you something you didn't know?” Watch people try the labs. Ask Sales which pages they actually use with customers and which explanations still aren't clicking.

### Did it change their behavior?

- **Did they take it back to their project?** Track skill downloads and bundles, then successful use in PostHog where we can connect those actions. Downloading a skill is a useful signal, but it isn't proof that someone used it successfully.
- **Can their agent do the thing?** Keep a small set of tasks for the priority topics. Check that an agent can find the right skill, use it, and verify its work. Run these again when the related references or skills change.
- **Is personalization useful?** Measure who uses “Learn with my project,” whether they continue with the content, and what they say about the result.
- **Is Twig actually improving?** Link real user activity to signals, proposed changes, and what happened after we shipped them. A daily PR quota won't tell us this.

We'll build the events and an initial dashboard with the platform, review them alongside user feedback every two weeks, and use the first results to set sensible targets. Connecting an anonymous lesson visit to an action in someone's project needs work; I won't assume that funnel already exists.

We should also watch reference access, search entry points, and signup conversion while changing docs. We can experiment with sending people to Learn instead of the current getting-started experience once both routes are ready. “Recover docs pageviews” isn't the goal, but breaking a useful route into PostHog would be a pretty bad outcome.

## Design

### Learn is where you choose your path

Docs 2.0 lives inside `/docs`. If someone has already found us there, we shouldn't make them go find a separate academy to learn the thing.

The Learn integration we have today mostly brings pocket guides into docs. The next step is to make each topic's Learn page a home for its learning paths. An AIO Learn page could look like this:

```
Learn AI observability

Read it          Explore it              Try it
Pocket guide     Inspect Twig's          Find and fix an
to AIO           AI instrumentation      expensive AI interaction

                    [ Learn with my project ]

Take it with you: [ Choose your skills ]
```

If you learn best through a use case, read the pocket guide. If you need to see where the instrumentation goes and what data comes out, explore Twig. If you want to make something and figure it out as you go, start a lab. These choices connect to each other, and you don't have to do all of them.

“Learn with my project” lets you sign in and choose your PostHog project before opening a guide or lab. That choice carries into the explanations and examples you read there. The home page is where you choose; the guide chapters and lab instructions are what get personalized. Exploring Twig still shows Twig and its data.

The reference pages stay easy to reach under the relevant tool. They link into Learn where there's a useful related path. A lab links back to the explanation it builds on. At the end, you get a bundle of relevant skills to take to your agent. No more “cool, I read the thing, now what?”

We'll keep one source for each piece of content. Existing pocket-guide URLs can remain as entry points while the same content becomes part of Learn. We don't need two copies of a book to do that.

Before building the rest, I'll mock the self-driving Learn page and the journey from explanation → Twig → lab → skills. That gives the website team something concrete to review, and us a structure to build the other paths on.

### Twig is so back, slightly differently

In the [original Twig RFC](https://github.com/PostHog/requests-for-comments-public/pull/593), I proposed a self-driving puzzle company. I'm changing that: **Twig becomes our deployed self-driving example app, and the app we build educational content on top of.**

Yes, this takes away some of the “this isn't just another demo” magic. But it gives us something we can use everywhere: docs, labs, sales conversations, conferences, and our own testing. Real people use the site, it produces real data, and we can show what self-driving actually does with it.

We already have a starting point in [Unter](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog.com/pull/18986) and [the HogPilled guide using it](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog.com/pull/19840). Unter pairs an app interaction with an explanation, the instrumentation behind it, and the data it produces. That's the bit we should build on.

The premise stays: **Uber for hedgehogs.** The mechanics get more grounded. Pick a destination, request a ride, see its status, ask for help. Someone shouldn't have to work out how cutting holes in fences constitutes a transport network before they can learn about PostHog.

Here's the work:

1. Adapt the existing Unter screens and components into those familiar interactions, using Twig's branding.
2. Deploy the app on twig.com and instrument its journeys. The rides are fictional; the people clicking around and the data from those interactions are real.
3. Add an inspect mode that connects an action to its instrumentation and resulting data. Decide which data is safe to show publicly before exposing it.
4. Connect self-driving, with me reviewing proposed changes to the deployed app. Keep a readable trail from the evidence to the PR and what happened after it shipped.
5. Use the same app components in guides and labs, so people recognize what they're looking at.

**The deployed app and the labs have different jobs.** Twig gives us real usage to explore. Labs give us a repeatable scenario someone can change, break, and reset. We'll label simulated data and keep lab activity separate from Twig's real signals.

The existing HogPilled story is illustrative. We can use it to teach, but its fictional outage doesn't become a real Twig case study because we changed the name. Real case studies come from what actually happens after deployment.

The signal-supply problem from the original RFC still matters. Putting a site online doesn't guarantee people come back. We'll bring people in through Learn, existing guides, Sales demos, and launch content, then watch what they do. The site needs to be worth exploring. Revenue and one PR per day aren't requirements for this version of Twig; useful activity and understandable improvements are.

### Labs: let me mess with it

I still like the tactile part of Codecademy. But the goal here isn't to recreate its editor and terminal. It's to let someone try a PostHog concept and see whether they understood it.

**The first iteration uses simulated controls.** You don't have to sign up for a separate PostHog project or point an agent at your repo to try it.

For example, want to learn feature flags?

1. Open a Twig lab where a new ride-booking option is behind a flag.
2. Change the targeting rule and see which test users get the option.
3. Try a scenario where the wrong users see it.
4. Inspect the evaluation, fix the rule, and check the result.
5. Get an explanation of why it worked, and a bundle of skills to apply the same concept to your own project.

Each lab has a starting state, a task, controls, checks, an explanation, and a reset. We validate the result, not just whether someone clicked the expected button. Each learner's changes stay in their own exercise, and nothing they do changes deployed Twig.

The content stays in MDX. The lab declares which components it needs: instructions, a Twig view, simulated PostHog controls, evidence to inspect. We can add new exercises without rebuilding the whole layout.

For self-driving, the learner's task might be to inspect a signal and decide whether the proposed fix addresses it. Their agent can do the implementation later. Knowing what to ask and how to check it is part of being hands-on.

If this works, we can expand into real isolated PostHog projects where learners make actual changes. First we need evidence that the simulation teaches something, and that people hit a useful limit we can remove with a real project.

### What we teach first

**Self-driving is the priority.** Then AIO, error tracking, session replay/replay vision, and the other high-value sources of self-driving signals.

I'm proposing four connected learning paths in Q4, building on existing content where we can:

| Path | Example we build around Twig | What you should leave knowing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-driving | A booking step breaks. Follow the evidence into a signal, a proposed fix, review, and validation. | What makes a useful signal, how to assess the change, and how to check that it helped. |
| AIO | Add an AI ride-help interaction to Twig and inspect a slow, expensive, or failed response. | How to read a trace, understand the cost, and use the evidence to investigate a problem. |
| Error tracking | A booking request fails. Find the error, its context, and the affected sessions. | How to understand the failure and its impact before trying to fix it. |
| Session replay/replay vision | A user struggles to choose a pickup point. Inspect the replay and an automated finding where the tool supports it. | How to understand the behavior, check the interpretation, and connect it to a useful signal. |

Each path gets an explanation, a Twig example, a simulated lab, and skills to take away. Personalization makes the relevant examples apply to your project. We'll confirm the exact scenarios against what the tools support before building them.

Surveys and booking-funnel drop-off are good candidates for supporting examples. We can add more sources as we find useful questions to teach through them.

Pocket guides remain a big part of this. They're narrative, illustrated with interactive components, and built around use cases. We should also add “what actually goes wrong” sections, sourced from real problems. The next step is to connect the guides to something you can inspect and do.

### Docs become skills

Ian's skills-library suggestion and the discussion around it are part of this plan. We shouldn't make someone choose between reading a reference, asking their agent to figure it out from docs, and implementing everything themselves.

Reference pages need to get significantly simpler. Trim repetition, get to the actual behavior and constraints, and keep examples that help someone use the tool correctly. Humans should still be able to read them. Agents should be able to crawl them without needing to operate an interactive page.

**Every reference page gets a “Download as skill” action.** And the docs site gets a library where you can mix and match what you download. Almost like Docker Hub for PostHog docs skills.

Want self-driving, error tracking, and session replay? Select them, see what's included, download the bundle. Finished an AIO lesson? Start with that lesson's suggested skills and add anything else you need.

How we build it:

- Keep reference content and its skill export tied to the same source. The skill adds the instructions and supporting references an agent needs to use it; a renamed Markdown file isn't automatically a good skill.
- Add metadata for the tool, task, description, and related skills. This drives the library and the suggested bundle at the end of a lesson.
- Package selected skills with the supporting material they need, without duplicating shared files.
- Check that every reference page has a working download, and that the downloaded content can actually be used outside the site.
- Maintain representative agent tasks to catch inaccurate or incomplete skills as the docs change.

The library starts with our own docs skills. Community publishing can be a separate idea later.

**What about the existing tutorials?** Ian's suggestion is a good starting point: inventory them, see what's still relevant, check the steps, and fix what's wrong. Preserve useful URLs and search traffic. Turn useful instructions into skills, move useful explanations into Learn, and redirect content we retire. Start the deeper accuracy work with the Q4 priorities; every-page download coverage doesn't magically mean every old tutorial has been rewritten.

### What if they still don't want to read?

That's fine! Their agent can read it for them.

Learn content is written for humans, but agents can still crawl it. The use cases, trade-offs, failure modes, and explanations are useful context for them too. Someone who never opens a pocket guide could still benefit when their agent finds it while working on their project.

For example: someone asks their agent to set up self-driving. The references explain how to configure it. The Learn content explains what makes a useful signal, when a proposed fix deserves a closer look, and how to check whether it helped. That's context the agent can use both to do the work and to explain its decisions to the human.

So we need to make the teaching accessible outside the interactive experience. Explanations, example inputs and outcomes, and lab walkthroughs should be readable as text, without clicking through a simulation. The related skills should point to that material too. This applies to the public content; personalized project data stays private.

The bet is that better teaching gives agents better context, which can help the people who only use agents anyway. We should test that with the same agent tasks we use to check our skills: does access to the Learn content improve the result and the explanation?

### Mock UI is the component library

Instead of screenshots (we will still sometimes need them, this isn't meant to be a replace all situation), docs use live replicas of PostHog UI, built as React components.

Models are really great at producing small, clean replicas from source or a DOM snapshot, _and_ the components are testable in a way PNGs aren't. Plus we get to remove noise instead of cropping around it... the pocket guides already do this and they look really slick.

But they still need maintaining. We should reuse actual PostHog components where it makes sense, and check the replicas against the behavior they're teaching.

So the plan is:

- Start with the existing pocket-guide figures, Unter components, and inspection controls.
- Pull the reusable pieces into a shared library: figures, annotations, simulated controls, lab steps, feedback, and reset.
- Keep Twig's app behavior separate from the controls that teach it.
- Use the same pieces in guides, labs, and personalized pages.
- Give each component an example and a check for the behavior it teaches, so updates don't silently break a lesson.

It's a small set we control. A second lesson using the same flag controls should mostly be content and configuration work.

### Personalized docs/agent-driven pages

This is the one I want BAD. Choose _“Learn with my project”_ on the Learn home page, select your PostHog project, then open a guide or lab. Its explanations and examples use your project's actual events, property names, and relevant configuration.

For example: you choose your project on the AIO Learn page, then open a chapter on understanding AI costs. That chapter explains the concept using relevant traces and costs from your project. If you open the related lab, its instructions can connect the exercise back to those examples while you practice with simulated controls.

Someone who lands directly on a guide chapter or lab gets the same “Learn with my project” control there. The selected project carries with them as they move between the learning paths, and they can switch back to the standard Twig examples at any time.

The work is:

1. Connect signed-in docs readers to projects they have access to. Start this early; it affects whether the rest can work.
2. Read the relevant context for the page they're on.
3. Adapt the explanation and examples using our existing components. As Danilo flagged, the agent should choose from a defined set of elements, not inject arbitrary page code.
4. Make the selected project visible and switchable. Keep the original page available.
5. If there's no useful data yet, say what's missing and fall back to the Twig example.

Reference content stays canonical. Personalization is a view over the page, and another person or crawler never gets your generated version.

Personalization doesn't turn the simulated lab into a live editor for your project. It helps you understand the lesson in your own context. The skill bundle is how you take the next step with your agent.

We'll build it into the priority learning paths in Q4, with an access review before release. Generating entire new courses is a different project.

### Knowledge sniffing agent

Trying to source knowledge gaps in sales means I have to manually read threads. The sales team also has to take their content and try to transform it into a format where it won't be siloed. This doesn't scale.

I'm proposing a **custom scout**: you tag @PostHog in an approved thread, and that thread becomes a draft for educational content.

What it does:

1. Reads the thread and the relevant linked material it has permission to access.
2. Checks whether we already explain the thing, or whether there's a draft for it.
3. Proposes an update, pocket-guide section, failure-mode explanation, or lab outline using the platform's content structure.
4. Includes the sources for the reviewer and removes customer-specific details from the public draft.
5. Opens a draft PR on posthog.com and replies in-thread with the link, docs is tagged for review. I'd like human reviews here, this is the stuff we shouldn't leave entirely to an agent imo.

The Q4 version starts with explicitly tagged threads. We can add proactive scanning of approved channels or call transcripts once the intake and review process works. The first useful test is straightforward: can Sales tag a real knowledge gap and get a useful draft that improves what we teach?

### Build it all in house

I don't think piece-mealing this with different providers is a good idea. Tying ourselves to an LMS vendor limits how far we can go with this.

The educational platform lives on posthog.com, in our repo, with our own components. Twig is the deployed app on twig.com. The shared components connect it back to the content and labs.

### Trade-offs

- **A deployed example app needs care.** We get real activity and reusable examples, but someone has to review changes, handle problems, and keep it useful. That's part of my Q4 work and its ongoing ownership.
- **Simulated labs need to be accurate.** They make it much easier to start learning, but a control that teaches the wrong behavior is worse than a screenshot. We'll check them against the real tool.
- **More connected content means fewer disconnected pieces.** Four complete learning paths is a clearer commitment than a pile of new pages. We can keep expanding the system after Q4.
- **Personalization has a real dependency.** Project access and permissions need work early, even though the finished experience ships later.
- **Skills and references need to stay in sync.** One source and automated export checks help; they don't replace checking that the instructions are useful.

## Sprints

I'm betting on this for Q4, we go all in. The platform, component library, and example app are things we'll keep building content on for years. Q4 is about getting the connected system shipped.

Here's the proposed sequence. These are planning estimates; I'll check them against the actual build work and the help available.

| Sprint | When | What ships |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Platform foundations | Weeks 1–2 | Self-driving Learn page design and the connected platform structure; shared components, lab format, skill format, and measurement. Resolve how we bring the existing Unter/HogPilled work in. |
| Deploy Twig fr | Weeks 3–5 | Twig deployed, inspect mode, simulated labs, and the connected self-driving path. First reference skill downloads. Start bringing real visitors in. |
| Skills, AIO, and error tracking | Weeks 6–8 | Downloads on every reference page, the mix-and-match skills library, AIO and error-tracking paths, and personalization using an accessible project. Reference simplification starts with these priority tools. |
| Replay and knowledge intake | Weeks 9–10 | Session replay/replay vision path, supporting signal-source examples, and the tagged-thread-to-draft knowledge agent. Extend personalization across the useful parts of the priority paths. |
| Make it work together, socialize it | Weeks 11–13 | Fix gaps found in the connected journeys, check skills and lab accuracy, finish access/accessibility reviews, run the Sales walkthrough and public launch, and review what people learned. |

Project-access work starts with the foundations, even though personalization appears later in the table. Reference cleanup, content writing, and knowledge-agent preparation happen alongside the platform work. Twig traffic starts early enough to give self-driving something to work with.

By the end of Q4, someone should be able to choose a path, understand it through Twig, practice it, adapt the examples to their project, and leave with useful skills. Sales should be able to use the same system and feed missing knowledge back into it.

### Out of scope

- **Certifications.** We aren't building an exam or proctoring system.
- **Video as the primary format.** We have a YouTube team for a reason. Supporting video is fine.
- **A paid LMS.** We're building this in-house.
- **Real isolated projects for every learner.** The first labs use simulated controls. We decide whether to expand after seeing people use them.
- **Running a separate games company.** Twig is the shared educational app. Payments, premium puzzles, and a revenue target aren't part of this plan.

### Getting it out there

No webinars, don't worry. But I do think we need to collab with community to get the content out there. Events, both in person and online, with the sole purpose of _learning something_ might be a hit.

The things I can commit to directly are links from the relevant docs and guides, an internal walkthrough for Sales, and launch content showing a complete learning path. If the events team has capacity, we can turn that same path into a workshop. We shouldn't need a separate curriculum every time we want to teach someone PostHog.
