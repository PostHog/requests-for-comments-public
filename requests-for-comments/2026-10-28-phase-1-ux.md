# RFC: Phase 1.0 of PostHog UX (aka product design direction)

### Defined terms
* Product: one of our apps or tools, such as MCP analytics or Feature flags.
* Silo: a product with clear boundaries, where it starts and where it ends.
* Rail item: an entry in the left new navigation rail, such as Analyze or People.
* View: one page or tab inside a product, such as MCP analytics' Tool quality.
* UX: the web user experience, meaning how a user interacts with our platform and how they feel about it.

## TL;DR
* Build out the rail: Home, Chats, Analyze, People, Release, Monitor, Automate, Data.
* Chats stays on the rail for now.
  We plan to move it into the AI side panel, which keeps the rail to Home plus the job items.
* Keep the Products rail item as it is in production.
  Every product keeps its own page, so people can still go straight to "MCP analytics".
* Also list each product's views under the rail item that matches the job they do.
  The rail links to the product's page; nothing is copied.
* Example: MCP analytics' dashboard sits in Analyze, its tool quality and notifications in Monitor, and its setup in Data.

## Problem statement
Today the only way into PostHog is through a product.
Before a user can solve a problem, they have to know:
* a) which products we have,
* b) which of them solves their problem, and
* c) how to use those products together.

This costs us in three ways:
* New users meet a list of product names before they meet their own problem.
* Work that spans products, like "ship a flag, watch for errors, check adoption", means jumping between silos.
  Nothing connects the steps.
* Users never find the products that would help them, because nothing puts those products in their path.

Products themselves aren't the problem.
They stay, and so do their pages.

The problem is that they are the only way in.

PostHog today is like a diner menu: you can order anything, but it takes a lot of effort just to get pancakes.
A minimal product is a tasting menu: pleasant, but restrictive.
We want each user to get their own tasting menu, picked from the full diner menu.

## Context
We have no strict design framework to build towards.
* Our navigation grew one product at a time.
  Each product solves a real user problem, but none of them knows about the others, or about what the user is trying to get done.
* Without an opinionated direction, the rail and everything inside it will go the same way.
  Every new product, idea or feature will be added wherever there's room.

## The Proposal
Break each product down into its views, and sort each view by the question it answers.
One product maps to one or more rail items.
Each view lives in exactly one rail item, and its link always opens the product's own page.

Each rail item answers one question:

* **Home**: What needs me today?
  An opinionated overview of what you care about, what you should care about, and what's happening.
* **Chats**: What have I asked PostHog AI to do?
  Every conversation you start, from anywhere in the app.
  This rail item is moving to the AI side panel later; see "Chats moves later".
* **Analyze**: What's happening, and why?
  Dashboards, insights, the SQL editor, activity, session replay, and each product's own dashboards, such as MCP analytics.
* **People**: Who are my users and customers, and how do I reach them?
  Persons, cohorts, accounts, the customer analytics dashboard and journeys, and the tools that talk to users: surveys, support tickets and messaging.
* **Release**: How do I change what my users get, and did it work?
  Feature flags, experiments and early access features.
* **Monitor**: Is anything broken, and will I hear about it?
  Error tracking, logs, alerts, and each product's health views, such as MCP analytics' tool quality.
* **Automate**: What should happen without me?
  Workflows, destinations, subscriptions, scouts and agent tasks.
* **Data**: What's coming in, what does it mean, and where does it go?
  Sources, the data warehouse, event and property definitions, pipelines, ingestion health, and each product's setup.

Home and Chats are ways in, not jobs.
The other six are the jobs.
A seventh job item needs to pass rule 3 below.

The items build on each other in a loop.
You notice something in Analyze or People, you ship a change in Release, you watch it in Monitor, you make it happen on its own in Automate, and you check the result back in Analyze.
Data feeds every step, and Home shows you what's next.

### Rules for placing a view
1. Place a view by the question it answers, not by the product it belongs to.
2. If a view fits two rail items, place it by its main action.
   Exploring, filtering or comparing goes in Analyze.
   Fixing something or setting an alert goes in Monitor.
3. No new rail item without evidence that it's a destination people return to.
   A new product finds its place inside the existing rail items.

### Why these job items
Each job item had to show that people go there on purpose and come back to it.
Automate is the exception, and its case is below.

* **People** is its own item, not part of Data.
  Persons is the fourth most visited destination, and about 40% of visits to it start there.
  People come back to it several days a week.
  Data pages are setup work, and their repeat use is a third or less of persons'.
  Putting persons in Data would bury a daily lookup in a pane built for occasional setup.
  The real split is the job, not the data type: "am I reading or setting up?", not "is it data?".
  People who use the tools that talk to users also opened persons in about half to 60% of cases.
* **Release** is its own item because feature flags have the highest share of visits that start there of anything measured, and most experiment visits come from flags.
* **Monitor** is the engineer's daily check.
  Each product in it is small on its own, but together they make a real area.
* **Data** is setup done now and then by fewer people.
  Browsing data stays in Analyze; defining it, or moving it in and out, is Data.

### Automate
_What should happen without me?_

Automate holds anything that acts on its own when a trigger or a schedule fires.
Two rules keep it apart from its neighbours:
* If it only tells you something, it's Monitor.
  An alert notices a problem; a workflow does something about it.
* If it changes what your users get in the product, it's Release.
  A scheduled flag change is part of shipping, so it stays with the flag.

Workflows is why this needs its own item.
Its triggers already go well past messaging: an event, a cohort join or leave, a survey response, a support ticket being created or changing status, an account tag changing, a GitHub or Slack event, a new row in a warehouse table, a batch of persons, or a manual run.
People can no longer hold it.

**What goes in it:** Workflows (with its Library, Channels, Opt-outs, Suppression list and Settings tabs), Transactional, Destinations, Subscriptions, Inbox scouts and agent tasks.
The appendix lists each one with its reason.

**What sounds automatic but stays where it is:**
* Alerts stay in Monitor, because they tell you and don't act.
* Scheduled flag changes and release scheduling stay in Release, because they belong with the flag.
* Broadcasts stay in People, because a person decides to send each one.
* Batch exports, warehouse destinations, transformations and ingestion filtering stay in Data, because they're plumbing.
* Pulse briefs stay in Home, because they're written for you to read.

#### Automate is a bet
Automate doesn't pass rule 3 yet.
Last week:
* Destinations reached about 1,660 orgs and 1,900 people. 6% came back on three or more days.
* Workflows reached about 910 orgs and 1,110 people. 14% came back on three or more days, the highest of the group.
  Its visits are deep: about 19 page views per person over two days, which looks like building.
* Inbox scouts reached about 560 orgs, Subscriptions about 380 and agent tasks about 120.
* Workflows, Destinations, batch exports and Subscriptions together reached about 2,600 orgs and 3,100 people, and 9% came back on three or more days.
  Adding scouts and tasks keeps the group under 3,300 orgs even if none of them overlap.

The bar the other job items met was about 5,000 orgs a week, with at least 15% coming back on three or more days.
Feature flags clear it at 5,400 orgs and 23%.
Automate reaches about half as many orgs, and fewer come back.

_Last 7 days to 16 Nov 2026, US cloud pageviews, internal users included. The batch exports path matched almost nothing, so their numbers may be undercounted._

We're adding it anyway, as a stated bet.
Workflows has outgrown People, and the job is growing: scouts, agent tasks and AI-driven workflows are all new.
These numbers are the baseline.
If Automate's reach and return don't grow toward the bar, it folds back, and its views return to where they were before.

### Chats moves later
Chats stays on the rail for now, and we plan to move it.

Chats is a way in, not a job.
A chat can be about anything, so it has no question of its own, and the AI side panel already opens from every page with the chat and its history.
Once Chats leaves, the rail is Home plus the job items.

It's also the busiest area we measured, so it can't move until chats stay one click away:
* About 13,100 orgs and 16,300 people opened `/ai` in the last week, against about 5,400 orgs for feature flags.
  Some of them may land there by default, so this overstates how many go there on purpose.
* About 9% of those people came back on three or more days, the same as Automate and well under feature flags' 23%.

_Same data window as above._

Where each Chats view goes when it moves:
* Conversations and Spaces move to the AI side panel.
  Chats still in progress show on Home.
* MCP servers and Skills move to Settings, next to PostHog AI's memory and instructions.
* Business knowledge moves to Data, because it describes what your business and data mean.
* Agent tasks have already moved to Automate.

Before it moves:
* "New chat" needs a spot as obvious as the rail tile, such as the top bar or Home.
* Check the chat history and long threads at side-panel width.

### What changes in today's Analytics pane
The Analytics pane becomes Analyze, which was the name it was meant to have.
* **By PostHog** started as a list of prebuilt PostHog dashboards.
  It now holds the ready-made analysis views PostHog gives you: Web analytics, Marketing analytics, MCP analytics, Session replay, Activity and Heatmaps.
  They're all "what's happening, and why?" views you didn't have to build, so they belong together under Analyze.
* **The Monitor section** splits up once the new rail items ship.
  Alerts move to Monitor, and Subscriptions move to Automate.
* Changes to this pane go through the product analytics team first, with the reason for each one.

#### Gaps we need to figure out
Each view below is placed in the list above, but the call could go either way.

* **"Destinations"** => Automate, or Data?
  because each destination reacts to events like a workflow does, but it's also the pipeline out of PostHog, next to batch exports.
  It's the biggest piece of Automate by reach: leaving it in Data shrinks Automate to about 2,000 orgs a week at most.
* **"Inbox › Scouts"** => Automate, or Home?
  because scouts run on their own, but splitting them from the reports they write may cost more than it gains.
* **"Cohorts"** => People, or Data?
  because you browse and reuse cohorts as groups of people, but a cohort is also a definition, like an action.
  Earlier drafts listed cohort definitions under Data.
* **"Product tours"** => Release, or People?
  because it's a change you ship into the product, but it talks to users.
* **"Surveys"** => People, or Analyze?
  because it talks to users, but its answers explain what your analytics can't.

## Open questions

* **Who owns Data?** The warehouse team has been working on a "Data warehouse" rail item with a Hex or Tableau style UX, now on hold behind other warehouse work.
  This RFC puts the warehouse inside a wider Data rail item, so the two need to become one plan, owned by one team.
  Ingestion, connectors, modeling and definitions should all sit in the same place.
* **Should AI observability sit in both Analyze and Monitor?** Being in two places breaks "each view lives in exactly one rail item", but the AI observability team has asked for it and session replays show users looking for it under Analytics.
  The cleaner answer is to split it into views the way MCP analytics splits.
  See the LLM analytics gap above.
* **Who owns Automate?** Workflows, Destinations and scouts belong to three different teams.
* **Release or Releases?** The RFC says Release, but the pane on master is labelled Releases.
* **Product analytics' alternative.** The product analytics team suggested the Persons and Groups tables and the Events explorer belong in Data, being closer to raw data, and the Customer analytics dashboard in Analyze's By PostHog section.
  "Why these job items" above gives the case for People, but the question is worth settling with them directly.

## What comes after phase 1

These ideas are out of scope for this RFC, but phase 1 shouldn't block them.

* **A Products landing page.** Clicking Products opens a page instead of only the sidebar: the products you use, then products we think you'd like with a short reason why, then everything else.
  Products becomes a place to find what you aren't using yet.
  This is where most upsells live.
* **Goal-led flows.** In a later phase, users start from a goal or problem rather than a product, and the UX walks them through the steps, suggesting products along the way.
* **A job-based rail.** An earlier exploration sorted the rail by the flow of data instead of by noun: Data in ("is my data arriving?"), Enrich ("what does it mean?"), Analyze ("what is going on, and why?"), Automate ("what should happen without me?") and Act ("what should I decide or do now?").
  Feedback was that the names are too abstract and hard to remember.
  The Automate job carried over into phase 1 as its own rail item.

## Success and kill criteria

I'll consider the test a success if:
* Users don't feel overwhelmed (feedback based).
* Users are able to reach for the tools they need through our semantic framing of the nav rail items.
* Users pick the right rail item first.
  We measure how often someone opens a rail item and then searches for, or jumps to, something that lives in another one.

We'll kill or rework it if:
* After a while, users continue to say "PostHog is confusing/overwhelming".
* Tool and product use is decreasing.
* Use across rail items (for example, a flag in Release followed by its errors in Monitor) is stagnant or decreasing.
* People keep opening the wrong rail item first, for example looking for Monitor views from Analyze.
* We dump things into each nav rail item without much thought (regression).
